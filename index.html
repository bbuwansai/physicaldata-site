      Minute by minute

      Clips capture actions. Episodes capture consequences.

      Human video already transfers to robots, and the best policies now remember minutes of context. The open problem is hours: what was started, what is waiting, where things were put. A clip is labeled for what happens inside it. It carries nothing about what it owes to the past. Scroll through one episode and watch the state build up.

              Where the gap is

              Acting is solved. Planning is close. Keeping track is not.

              A frontier model can write a good plan for a kitchen. The hard part is being 87 minutes into it and knowing the tray is still soaking, one glass is on a temporary shelf, and the stove was left half wiped when the trash filled up.

              Short, clean demonstrations. Well served by existing data.

              Keep track

              What happened, what changed, what is still owed?

              A grounded state of the world, maintained while acting in it. This is the data we make.

              What we record

              Not hours of footage. Events, hours apart, that depend on each other.

              Three hours of unrelated actions teaches nothing about memory. A 45-minute episode with a delayed consequence, an interruption, a retrieval, a plan change and a recovery teaches a lot. So we design every episode for those, and we annotate the state, not just the actions.

              Delayed consequences

              Something done now matters later. The tray soaks, the oven heats, the boxes wait by the door. Every episode contains several of these, 15 minutes to two hours apart, with both ends labeled and linked.

              Interruptions and resumption

              Real work is start A, interrupt for B, notice C, finish B, resume A. We record the suspended goal, the resume point and the return, so a model learns to pick up where it left off.

              Temporary placements and retrieval

              The cupboard is full, so the glasses wait on a shelf. Two hours later they come back. The placement opens an obligation; the retrieval closes it, and the two records point at each other.

              Failure and recovery

              The state after a mistake is one that clean demonstrations never reach. We keep the states successful demos never visit, and the paths that return from them. Nothing is re-shot.

              Same decision, different runs

              The same job in the same kitchen, 24 times, with the starting state changed on purpose. You get a distribution of decisions from similar situations, not one lucky run.

              The test

              One experiment settles it. We run it on your free episode.

              Nobody has shown whether keeping the continuity beats cutting the same footage into clips. It is a clean question, and you should not have to take our word for it.

              A

              Short clips

              The same footage cut into 30 to 60 second clips, shuffled. No history.

              B

              Clips with a summary

              The same clips, each with a written summary of what came before.

              C

              The full episode

              Full continuity plus our decision, obligation and state annotations.

              • What remains unfinished?
              • Where is the tray now, and why?
              • What should I resume after this interruption?
              • Which earlier action matters right now?
              • Has this subgoal already been completed?
              • What changed that made the plan wrong?

              Ask a capable model these questions at moments where the current frame is not enough to answer. Compare the three arms as the distance from the relevant event grows.

              If A and C score the same, the thesis is weak. If C wins, and wins by more as the gap widens, this data does something clips cannot.

              Whether that carries into robot task success is the second stage, and yours to run. The first stage needs no robot.

              How an episode is made

              Designed, recorded, repeated, annotated.

              01

              Design

              You pick the job and the environment. We design the episode to contain delayed consequences, at least one interruption, temporary placements and room for things to go wrong. 45 minutes to 4 hours, whatever the dependencies need.

              02

              Rig

              A head camera and one on each wrist, time-synced. Light enough that nobody works differently for wearing it.

              03

              Record

              The whole job, no cuts. Collectors mark decision points as they happen and otherwise work normally. When something goes wrong, they keep going. Nothing is re-shot.

              04

              Repeat

              The same home again with the starting state changed: a full cupboard, a blocked shelf, a person in the way. Then other homes. Twenty or more runs per place is normal.

              05

              Annotate

              Every decision point into a fixed schema, every obligation linked to its resolution, a state snapshot at each branch, subtask spans, hand keypoints. Checked by a second person before it ships.

              Formats
              RLDS, LeRobot, or the schema you already use. We match your existing data before we scale.
              Decision records
              One per branch, in a fixed schema. Marked by the collector at the moment, written up afterwards, never invented from the video alone.
              State
              A snapshot at every decision point: what is done, what is suspended, what is owed, what is where. This is the supervision signal for memory and resumption.
              Links
              Every obligation resolved to the record that closes it, every failure to its recovery, every interruption to its resume point.
              Quality
              Every episode reviewed by a second person. Sync drift, dropped frames and unannotated stretches are listed in meta.json, not hidden.
              Consent and privacy
              Written consent from the collector and the household. Bystander faces and documents blurred. Stricter rules on request.

              FAQ

              Things people ask on the first call.

              Isn't this already covered by the big human-video datasets?
              Partly, and we are glad they exist: they are the proof that human first-person video transfers to robots. They are far bigger and more diverse than anything we will collect, and they are clip-scale. We are not competing on scale. We design episodes so causes and consequences sit far apart in time, and we annotate the state in between. If you already have millions of clips, this is the data those clips do not contain.
              Do collectors talk the whole time?
              No. Continuous narration changes how people work and most of it is noise. Collectors mark decision points as they happen and say a sentence at the branch. We write each one up afterwards into a fixed schema.
              Do you cut out the mistakes?
              No. A dropped glass, a wrong cupboard, a plan that had to change: it stays in, and the recovery is linked to it. If you want a clean-only cut as well, we can deliver both.
              Who are the collectors?
              People we recruit, train and pay ourselves, working in their own or partner homes. Our team is based in India, which is what makes repeated long episodes affordable. We can run in other countries when a project needs it.
              Can we choose the jobs and the environments?
              Yes. Kitchens, laundry, whole-apartment cleaning, packing, restocking, a workshop, a small shop. You choose the job, the starting states you want varied, and how many homes and repeats. If a job is unusual we will tell you honestly whether we can do it well.
              How fast can we see data?
              A sample episode within two weeks of a brief, with the A/B/C comparison. After that, delivery runs weekly.
              How is it priced?
              Per delivered episode, with volume tiers. The sample is free. We put a number in writing before you commit to anything.
