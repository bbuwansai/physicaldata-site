Physical work has a history. Robot training data should too.

Most demonstrations capture an action. We capture the chain of events that made the action necessary: the tray left soaking, the glasses put somewhere temporary, the plan that changed when the trash filled up. Complete human task episodes, recorded first-person, with the state in between annotated, so a model at minute 120 still knows what happened at minute 12.

      The gap

      A 30-second clip cannot tell a model any of this.

      Clip datasets of human physical work are now large, and they clearly help robots generalize. What they cut out is the time between clips. These are the questions a model needs the history to answer.

      Three questions a robot has to answer

      Planning is mostly solved. Keeping track is not.

      Frontier models can already write a good plan for a kitchen. The hard part is being 87 minutes into that plan and knowing the tray is still soaking, one glass is on a temporary shelf, and the stove was left half wiped when the trash filled up.

      What we design into every episode

      We are not selling hours. We are selling events, hours apart, that still depend on each other.

      A three-hour recording of a thousand unrelated actions is nearly worthless for this. A 45-minute episode with a delayed consequence, an interruption, a memory retrieval, a plan change and a recovery is not. So we design the episodes, and we annotate the state, not just the actions.

      Delayed consequences.

      An action now creates an obligation later. The tray soaks, the oven heats, the boxes wait by the door. Every episode contains several causes whose consequences arrive 15 minutes to two hours later, and both ends are labeled and linked.

      Interruptions and resumption.

      Real work is start A, interrupt for B, notice C, finish B, resume A. We label the suspended goal, the resume point and the moment of return, so a model can learn to pick up where it left off instead of starting over.

      Temporary placements and retrieval.

      The cupboard is full, so the glasses go on a shelf for now. Two hours later they have to come back. Each placement is recorded as an open obligation, and the retrieval is linked back to it, which is what an episodic memory has to learn.

      Failure, diagnosis, recovery.

      The state after a mistake is one that clean demonstrations never reach. We collect the states successful demonstrations never visit, and the trajectories that return from them. Nothing is re-shot.

      Same decision point, different runs.

      The same job in the same kitchen, 24 times, with the initial state varied on purpose. The result is a distribution of decisions from similar situations, not more footage of one lucky run.

      How a decision point is annotated

      Not a running commentary. A record at every branch.

      Asking people to narrate continuously changes how they work and produces noise. Collectors mark decision points as they happen; we annotate each one afterwards into a fixed schema, and link it to the record it depends on.

      The experiment

      One test decides whether this is worth your money.

      Nobody has shown that preserving hours of continuity beats chopping the same footage into short clips. That is the question. It is falsifiable, and we run it with you on the free sample before you commit to anything.

      A

      Short clips

      The same footage cut into 30 to 60 second clips, shuffled. No episode history.

      B

      Episode-aware clips

      The same clips, each with a summary of the preceding state and task history.

      C

      Continuous episode

      Full temporal history plus our decision, obligation and state annotations.

      • What remains unfinished?
      • Where is the tray now, and why?
      • What should the agent resume after this interruption?
      • Which earlier action matters right now?
      • Has this subgoal already been completed?
      • What changed that invalidated the original plan?

      Ask a capable model these questions at points in the episode where the current frame is not enough to answer. Then compare the three arms as the distance from the relevant event grows.

      If A and C perform the same, the thesis is weak. If C beats A, and the gap widens with temporal distance, this data is doing something clips cannot.

      The second stage, whether that carries into robot task success, is yours to run. The first stage needs no robot at all.

      How an episode is run

      Designed, recorded, repeated, annotated.

      01

      Design

      You tell us the job and the environment. We design the episode so it contains delayed consequences, at least one interruption, temporary placements and room for things to go wrong. 45 minutes to 4 hours, whatever the dependencies need.

      02

      Rig

      A head camera and one on each wrist, time-synced. Light enough that nobody works differently because they are wearing it.

      03

      Record

      The collector does the whole job, no cuts. They mark decision points as they happen and otherwise work normally. When something goes wrong, they keep going. Nothing is re-shot.

      04

      Repeat

      The same home again with the initial state changed: a full cupboard, a blocked shelf, a person in the way. Then other homes. Twenty or more runs per place is normal.

      05

      Annotate

      Every decision point into a fixed schema, every obligation linked to its resolution, state snapshots at each branch, subtask spans, hand keypoints. Checked by a second person before it ships.

      Formats
      RLDS, LeRobot, or the schema you already use. We match your existing data before we scale.
      Decision records
      One per branch, in a fixed schema. Marked by the collector at the moment, written up afterwards, never invented from the video alone.
      State
      A snapshot at every decision point: what is done, what is suspended, what is owed, what is where. This is the supervision signal for memory and resumption.
      Links
      Every obligation resolved to the record that closes it, every failure to its recovery, every interruption to its resume point.
      Quality
      Every episode reviewed by a second person. Sync drift, dropped frames and unannotated stretches are listed in meta.json, not hidden.
      Consent and privacy
      Written consent from the collector and the household. Bystander faces and documents blurred. Stricter rules on request.

      Straight answers

      Things people ask on the first call.

      Isn't this already covered by large human-video datasets?
      Partly, and we are glad they exist: they are the evidence that human first-person data transfers to robots. They are also far bigger and more diverse than anything we will collect, and they are clip-scale. We are not competing on scale. We design episodes so that causes and consequences are far apart in time, and we annotate the state in between. If you already have millions of clips, this is the data those clips do not contain.
      Do collectors talk the whole time?
      No. Continuous narration changes how people work and most of it is noise. Collectors mark decision points as they happen; we annotate each one afterwards into a fixed schema. Short spoken notes at the branch are kept and transcribed, not a running commentary.
      Do you cut out the mistakes?
      No. A dropped glass, a wrong cupboard, a plan that had to change: it stays in, and the recovery is linked to it. If you want a clean-only cut as well, we can deliver both.
      Who are the collectors?
      People we recruit, train and pay ourselves, working in their own or partner homes. Our team is based in India, which is what makes repeated long episodes affordable. We can run in other countries when a project needs it.
      Can we choose the jobs and the environments?
      Yes. Kitchens, laundry, whole-apartment cleaning, packing, restocking, a workshop, a small shop. You choose the job, the initial states you want varied, and how many homes and repeats. If a job is unusual we will tell you honestly whether we can do it well.
      How fast can we see data?
      A sample episode within two weeks of a brief, with the A/B/C comparison. After that, delivery runs weekly.
      How is it priced?
      Per delivered episode, with volume tiers. The sample is free. We will put a number in writing before you commit to anything.
